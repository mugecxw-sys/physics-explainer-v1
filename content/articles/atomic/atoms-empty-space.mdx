<ShortAnswer>
Atoms have tiny nuclei compared with their overall size, so it is common to say that atoms are “mostly empty space.” But that phrase can be misleading.

The region outside the nucleus is not an empty classical shell. It is where electrons are described by extended quantum states, and those electrons interact electromagnetically with electrons and nuclei in nearby atoms.

When two pieces of matter are pushed together, their electron distributions begin to overlap. Electric interactions change the energy, and the **Pauli exclusion principle** restricts how identical electrons can share quantum states. Compressing the matter further therefore becomes energetically costly.

That rising energy produces the forces we experience as resistance to compression and contact.

Matter is not solid because atoms are tiny hard balls. Solidity comes from quantum mechanics, electromagnetism, and the bonding structure of matter.
</ShortAnswer>
## Why “Mostly Empty Space” Sounds Like a Paradox

A typical atomic nucleus is extremely small compared with the size of the atom.

That is why diagrams often show a tiny central nucleus surrounded by a much larger electron region.

From that picture, a natural question follows:

> If almost all of the atom's volume is not occupied by the nucleus, why doesn't my hand pass through a table?

The hidden assumption is that “not nucleus” means “nothing there.”

That is not the quantum picture.

An atom is not a hard nucleus sitting inside a hollow container. Its electrons are described by wavefunctions spread through regions of space around the nucleus. Those quantum states determine how the atom interacts with other atoms.

So “mostly empty space” is useful only in one limited sense:

> The nucleus occupies a very small fraction of an atom's volume.

It should not be taken to mean:

> Most of an atom is a completely inactive void through which other atoms can freely move.

## Electron Clouds Are Not Hard Shells

You may have seen an atom drawn as a nucleus surrounded by a fuzzy cloud.

That cloud is not a material shell with a hard outer surface.

It represents the spatial structure of electron quantum states—often visualized using probability density.

An electron in an atom is not assigned one fixed classical path around the nucleus. Its state extends over a region of space.

When two atoms approach, these electron states begin to interact and overlap.

At that point, the energy of the combined system changes.

This is where the idea of two mostly empty objects simply sliding through each other breaks down. The quantum states are not independent empty containers. Bringing the atoms together changes the allowed states and the electromagnetic energy of the system.

## Electromagnetic Interactions Matter

Atoms contain charged particles:

- positively charged nuclei,
- negatively charged electrons.

The electromagnetic interaction between those charges is central to atomic structure and chemical bonding.

At ordinary distances, attractive interactions can hold atoms together in molecules and solids. Different materials do this in different ways—covalent bonding, ionic bonding, metallic bonding, and weaker intermolecular interactions all have different structures.

But if atoms are pushed too close together, the energy rises sharply.

Part of that comes from ordinary electric interactions:

- electron-electron repulsion,
- nucleus-nucleus repulsion,
- electron-nucleus attraction.

The total result is not captured by the slogan “electrons repel each other.”

Matter can be stable only because attractive and repulsive contributions, together with quantum restrictions, produce preferred arrangements and equilibrium separations.

That is why a crystal can have a definite lattice spacing instead of either exploding apart or collapsing into one point.

## Where the Pauli Exclusion Principle Enters

Electrons are **fermions**.

Identical fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle: they cannot all occupy the same quantum state.

For electrons in atoms, this is often taught as:

> No two electrons in the same atom can have the same complete set of quantum numbers.

The deeper many-particle statement comes from the antisymmetric quantum state required for identical fermions.

Why does this matter when two objects touch?

As the electron states associated with neighboring atoms overlap more strongly, the electrons cannot simply pile into the same already-occupied low-energy states.

The combined many-electron wavefunction has to rearrange while respecting fermionic antisymmetry.

That rearrangement generally raises the energy when ordinary matter is compressed strongly.

A force is related to how energy changes with position. If pushing atoms closer causes the energy to rise steeply, the system pushes back.

That quantum contribution is a major reason ordinary matter resists compression.

## Pauli Exclusion Is Not a Tiny Repulsive Force

It is common to hear:

> “The Pauli exclusion force keeps atoms apart.”

That phrase is convenient, but it can create the wrong picture.

Pauli exclusion is not a new fundamental force carried by a special particle.

It is a restriction on the allowed quantum states of identical fermions.

The resulting energy cost can produce an effective resistance that behaves like a repulsion in many situations, but the underlying statement is about quantum-state structure.

This distinction matters because the solidity of real materials is not produced by one universal “Pauli force.”

Electromagnetic interactions determine bonding and much of the energy landscape, while fermionic quantum statistics strongly constrain how electrons can occupy states when atoms overlap.

Both pieces matter.

## So What Happens When Your Hand Touches a Table?

At everyday scale, your hand and the table each contain enormous numbers of atoms.

As you press downward, the atoms at the surfaces approach one another.

Their electron distributions interact electromagnetically and begin to overlap. The allowed many-electron states of the combined system must obey the Pauli principle. Pushing the atoms closer raises the total energy very rapidly.

The result is an upward force from the table on your hand.

You feel that as contact.

There is no need for the nuclei in your hand to physically collide with the nuclei in the table.

In normal contact, the relevant atomic distances are vastly larger than nuclear sizes.

Your hand is stopped because the quantum-electromagnetic energy of forcing those atomic structures through one another becomes enormous long before the nuclei could simply pass through each other.

## Why Doesn't Quantum Tunneling Let You Pass Through?

Quantum particles can sometimes appear on the other side of a barrier that classical physics says they cannot cross. This is called **quantum tunneling**.

So could every particle in your hand tunnel through the table?

For one microscopic particle and a thin barrier, tunneling can be measurable.

For a macroscopic object containing an astronomical number of interacting particles, the situation is radically different.

Passing your whole hand through a table while preserving its structure would require an extraordinarily coordinated quantum process involving a huge number of degrees of freedom. The amplitude for such a macroscopic event is so fantastically small that it is not a realistic everyday possibility.

Quantum mechanics does not say “anything can happen with a reasonable chance.”

It gives quantitative probabilities, and many formally possible processes are effectively never observed on ordinary timescales.

## Is Matter Really “Empty,” Then?

It depends on what you mean by empty.

If you mean:

> Is most of an atom's volume occupied by the dense nuclear matter found in the nucleus?

No. The nucleus is tiny compared with the atom.

If you mean:

> Is the rest of the atom an inert classical vacuum with nothing physically relevant happening there?

Also no.

The electron's quantum state extends through that region. Electric fields and quantum correlations matter there. When another atom approaches, the combined quantum state and interaction energy change.

So “atoms are mostly empty space” is a memorable scale comparison, not a complete description of atomic matter.

The phrase becomes misleading when it encourages us to picture atoms as tiny solid dots separated by literal nothingness.

## Why Some Solids Are Harder Than Others

If Pauli exclusion and electromagnetism apply to all ordinary matter, why is diamond hard while rubber bends?

Because **solidity** and **hardness** are not the same property.

Quantum mechanics and electromagnetism explain why atoms form stable structures and strongly resist being compressed into the same states.

The detailed mechanical behavior of a material depends on how its atoms and molecules are bonded and arranged.

A material can:

- stretch by changing molecular conformations,
- bend through changes in bond angles,
- deform by defects moving through a crystal,
- fracture when bonds break,
- resist compression very strongly.

So the fact that your hand cannot pass through either rubber or diamond has a common microscopic foundation, while their very different hardness comes from their different structures and bonding.

In both cases, the classical picture fails because matter at atomic scales is not made of tiny hard particles following ordinary trajectories.

Atomic size, electron structure, bonding, and resistance to compression all depend on quantum states.

Once that is understood, “mostly empty space” stops sounding like a reason that solid objects should pass through one another.

## One Thing to Remember

**Atoms are not mostly empty in the sense of being mostly physically irrelevant. Their nuclei are tiny, but their electron quantum states extend across the atomic scale. When atoms are forced together, electromagnetic interactions and fermionic quantum restrictions make further overlap energetically expensive, producing the resistance we experience as solid contact.**
