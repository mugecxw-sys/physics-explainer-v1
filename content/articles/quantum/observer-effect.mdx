<ShortAnswer>
In quantum mechanics, observing a system means making it interact with something that can record information about it. That interaction can change the system. The “observer” does not have to be a person looking at an experiment.
</ShortAnswer>

## Why this seems confusing

Everyday observation feels passive. Looking at a parked car does not seem to alter where the car is. At quantum scales, however, obtaining information requires a physical interaction, and that interaction can matter.

## What counts as a measurement?

A measurement is an interaction that links a property of the system to a record: a detector click, a mark on a screen, or another physical change that can carry information. The important step is the interaction, not the later moment when a person reads the result.

<VisualExplanation title="Interaction, record, result">
  A useful visual model is a three-step path: the system interacts with an apparatus, the apparatus creates a stable record, and the result can then be read.
</VisualExplanation>

## Does an observer have to be human?

No. In the language of physics, an observer can be a detector or another physical system. Conscious attention is not part of the standard laboratory definition of measurement.

<CommonMisconception>
“A conscious person creates reality by looking” is not what physicists usually mean by the observer effect. It replaces a physical interaction with a claim about the mind.
</CommonMisconception>

## What about consciousness?

Interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree about how to describe measurement at the deepest level. That open interpretive question should not be confused with the practical fact that experiments work without requiring a conscious observer inside the measurement process.

## One thing to remember

In quantum mechanics, “observation” is a physical interaction that produces information—not simply an act of human awareness.
