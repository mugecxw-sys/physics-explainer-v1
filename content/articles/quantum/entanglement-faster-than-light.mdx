<ShortAnswer>
Entangled measurements can be correlated across large distances, but each local result is individually unpredictable. Because neither side can choose its result, neither side can encode a controllable message in the correlation.
</ShortAnswer>

## Correlation is not communication

Two observers can later compare their records and discover a pattern stronger than classical physics allows. Before that comparison, each observer sees a random-looking sequence. A pattern visible only after ordinary communication cannot itself carry a faster-than-light message.

## Why choosing the measurement does not solve it

Changing which measurement is performed affects the joint statistics that appear when both data sets are compared. It does not produce a locally readable signal telling the distant observer what choice was made.

<VisualExplanation title="Two local records, one later comparison">
  Each side receives an unpredictable local result. The entanglement becomes evident when the two records are brought together through a normal communication channel.
</VisualExplanation>

## What the distant observer can see

The distant observer cannot distinguish, from local data alone, whether a measurement was performed elsewhere or which setting was chosen. There is no controllable change that can be read as a bit of information.

<CommonMisconception>
“The particles communicate instantly” is a tempting story, but it suggests a usable signal that the experiment does not provide. Entanglement is better described through joint predictions than through hidden messages exchanged between particles.
</CommonMisconception>

## One thing to remember

Entanglement gives nonclassical correlations, but communication requires a controllable local signal—and entanglement does not provide one.
