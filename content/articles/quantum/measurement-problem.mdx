<ShortAnswer>
Quantum theory describes a system as a combination of possible outcomes, yet an experiment records one definite result. The measurement problem asks how—or whether—the theory explains that transition.
</ShortAnswer>

## Two rules that seem hard to fit together

Between measurements, the quantum state evolves in a continuous, predictable mathematical way. A measurement, by contrast, appears to select a particular result according to probabilities. The tension between those descriptions is the core of the problem.

## Why probability is not the whole puzzle

The difficulty is not merely that quantum mechanics predicts probabilities. The deeper question is why a measurement has one experienced outcome when the mathematical state can include several alternatives.

<VisualExplanation title="Possibilities and a recorded outcome">
  Picture several allowed branches before a measurement and one stable record afterward. The measurement problem concerns how those descriptions belong to one theory.
</VisualExplanation>

## Is collapse a physical event?

Some interpretations treat collapse as a real process. Others treat it as an update in description, deny that a unique collapse occurs, or add further structure to the theory. Experiments constrain the predictions, but they do not automatically settle every interpretive account.

<CommonMisconception>
The measurement problem is not proof that quantum mechanics fails experimentally. Quantum predictions are extraordinarily successful; the problem concerns what the formalism says is happening when outcomes become definite.
</CommonMisconception>

## One thing to remember

The measurement problem is the gap between a quantum description containing alternatives and the single result recorded in an experiment.
